My AI safety research asks how we can tell what a model is actually doing, and how to catch it when that isn't what we intended. I work mostly in mechanistic interpretability and model auditing, in small, fully inspectable experiments. Every repository is public and reproducible, and the negative results are published alongside the positive ones.

Research threads

  • Automated mechanism discovery with SPD. A seven-repository series on clustering Stochastic Parameter Decomposition components into mechanisms. On toy models, gate co-activation recovers the true mechanisms. On a real transformer that grokked modular addition, components fail to specialise, and no clustering signal recovers the verified structure. The conclusion, specialization is the bottleneck, redirects effort toward component specialization before clustering design: sharper L0 gates, longer or stronger sparsity schedules, and per-mechanism capacity.
  • Universality of deception features. Do different architectures represent manipulation cues the same way? With an informative null, cross-architecture similarity was statistically significant (p = 0.024). The first, weaker test was reported as inconclusive rather than oversold.
  • Reward generalization. I reproduced a reward spreading to contexts it was never given in, then tested whether internal signals could warn before the behaviour appeared. At toy scale they couldn't, which sharpens what an early-warning system would need.
  • Auditing hidden objectives. At Apart Research's Secret Loyalties Hackathon I audited three model organisms weights-first. I found which were fine-tuned and where, rejected four tempting false positives with base-rate controls, and placed in the top half of 179 submissions.
